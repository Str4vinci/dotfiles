# Developmental Editing and Narrative Economy

Use this reference for full-manuscript audits and revisions centred on density, cuts, cohesion, repetition, redundancy, section function, or prose that merely reads a table or figure aloud.

## Editorial stance

- Be direct, specific, and evidence-based.
- Diagnose patterns rather than collecting isolated imperfections.
- Ground major criticism in identifiable sections, paragraphs, figures, tables, or recurring constructions.
- Quote only enough text to identify the issue.
- Do not praise passages merely to balance criticism. Identify effective material when its function explains why it should be preserved.
- Preserve deliberate voice, rhythm, ambiguity, qualification, and technical terminology unless they interfere with the intended effect.
- Distinguish scientific complexity from avoidable textual density.
- State uncertainty when evidence, provenance, journal requirements, or authorial intent is unclear.

## Order of operations

1. Confirm the current manuscript version, requested scope, journal constraints, and whether the task is read-only.
2. Verify central quantities, comparison bases, terminology, and section-to-section consistency where source evidence is available.
3. Resolve factual contradictions, missing provenance, and Methods–Results mismatches before compressing.
4. Map the function of every major section and assign each recurring claim a canonical home.
5. Diagnose density, cohesion, repetition, and redundancy at progressively smaller levels.
6. Apply section-level and paragraph-level revisions before line edits.
7. Recheck every retained number, qualifier, comparator, and scope after revision.
8. Complete production and formatting checks only after scientific and structural edits stabilise.

## Claim ownership

Use section ownership to prevent the manuscript from saying the same thing three times.

| Component | Primary function | Appropriate detail | Common duplication to remove |
|---|---|---|---|
| Abstract | State the question, essential method, principal evidence, and directional conclusion. | A small number of decision-defining quantities and one necessary scope boundary. | Secondary matrices, complete sensitivity ranges, and caveats already explained in the main text. |
| Introduction | Establish context, gap, objectives, and research questions. | Literature and concepts needed to motivate the study. | Results previews and Methods detail that do not sharpen the question. |
| Methods | Define design, scope, comparators, procedures, assumptions, and accounting. | Enough detail for interpretation and reproduction. | Results, repeated policy definitions, and limitations copied verbatim into several subsections. |
| Results | Present evidence in an argument-led sequence. | Patterns, decision boundaries, extrema, reversals, exceptions, mechanisms supported by analysis, and enough numerical anchors for scale. | Cell-by-cell narration of figures or tables and repeated setup owned by Methods. |
| Discussion | Interpret mechanisms, literature context, implications, and limitations. | Explanations and bounded inferences. | A second numerical Results section or unsupported generalisation. |
| Conclusion | Answer the research questions and state practical implications and uncertainty boundaries. | Few headline quantities only where they define the decision. | A subsection-by-subsection recap or complete repetition of the Abstract. |
| Supporting Information | Preserve exhaustive matrices, validation detail, secondary cases, and reproducibility material. | Stable cell-level values and procedures needed for auditability. | Main conclusions that readers need in the article itself. |

Keep a short local qualification where omission would invite misinterpretation, even if a fuller limitation is canonical elsewhere.

## Density analysis

Evaluate density at manuscript, section, paragraph, and sentence levels.

### Over-dense passages

Flag passages that:

- introduce several scopes, comparison bases, variables, or claims before any can be contextualised;
- combine results, qualifications, and interpretation in one long sentence;
- require the reader to map several values back through `respectively`;
- place multiple mechanisms or abstractions in competition;
- are difficult because the prose is compressed, not because the science is intrinsically complex.

Recommended actions include dividing, reordering, defining earlier, adding a transition, or expanding a missing mechanism.

### Low-yield passages

Flag passages that:

- repeat information already established;
- describe procedure after the reader has seen the result;
- explain an inference already made obvious by a strong sentence, figure, or table;
- narrate stable cells without identifying a pattern or consequence;
- continue after the central result or qualification has landed.

Recommended actions include cutting, compressing, merging, relocating, or replacing with an exact figure, table, or Supporting Information pointer.

For each substantial example, report:

- location;
- density type;
- reader effect;
- recommended action;
- content or function that must be preserved.

Also identify passages that genuinely need more space, especially missing definitions, comparator changes, mechanisms, transitions, and uncertainty boundaries.

## Prevent prose tables

A paragraph probably reads like a table when it contains three or more parallel comparisons, long strings of values, repeated scenario labels, several uses of `respectively`, or no inference that could not be obtained by inspecting the adjacent visual.

Do not delete all quantitative anchors. Rewrite around:

1. the governing pattern;
2. the decision-defining magnitude or boundary;
3. the most informative reversal, exception, or extremum;
4. the mechanism or comparator effect;
5. the scope qualification;
6. an exact visual or Supporting Information pointer for complete values.

A compact Results paragraph often follows this sequence:

> Finding or classification. Representative evidence. Exception or reversal. Mechanism or implication. Scope boundary.

Prefer one representative comparison over several examples proving the same point. Retain additional examples only when they establish distinct mechanisms, boundaries, or transferability.

## Potential cuts

Classify recommendations consistently:

- **Safe cut**: removable with little or no downstream effect.
- **Compression candidate**: useful content that should be shorter.
- **Structural cut**: a section, analysis, example, or digression that may not justify its space.
- **Relocation candidate**: useful content placed in the wrong section.
- **Conditional cut**: removable only if another passage, visual, or Supporting Information item assumes its function.

For important cuts, use:

| Location | Material or function | Recommendation | Cut level | Approximate words removable | Consequence or required adjustment |
|---|---|---|---|---:|---|

Estimate conservative and aggressive total cut ranges. Explain what additional relocation or Supporting Information work the aggressive range requires.

Do not recommend cuts merely because material does not advance a narrow result. Preserve text that advances mechanism, reproducibility, context, atmosphere, interpretation, uncertainty, or authorial purpose.

## Cohesion and continuity

Assess both the local defect and its manuscript-level consequence. Check:

- whether the opening questions match the later analytical priorities;
- whether Methods describe the analyses actually reported;
- whether definitions and scope labels appear before they are needed;
- whether comparison bases change without explicit notice;
- whether sections follow a causal or logical progression;
- whether important findings are prepared, earned, and followed through;
- whether caveats appear at the point where overgeneralisation becomes possible;
- whether threads, promised analyses, citations, or placeholders disappear without resolution;
- whether adjacent sections perform the same structural function and should be merged;
- whether the Conclusion answers the opening questions.

Do not allow smoother prose to conceal a missing result, stale method, contradictory scope, or unsupported interpretation. Mark unresolved conflicts with `[CHECK]`.

## Repetition and overused patterns

Count meaningful repetitions when feasible and provide representative locations. Separate:

- necessary technical terminology;
- intentional comparison scaffolds;
- harmless stylistic signatures;
- exact verbal repetition;
- semantic repetition, where different words perform the same explanatory function;
- structural repetition across Abstract, Results, Discussion, and Conclusion.

Treat watchlists as revision aids, not banned-word lists. Common scientific patterns worth checking include:

| Pattern | Check for | Typical action |
|---|---|---|
| Repeated sentence-opening `Under X` | Consecutive scenario-led sentences with identical rhythm. | Front the finding, consolidate conditions, or move the condition later. |
| `remained` | Use as a generic substitute for `was`. | Retain for genuine persistence across a changed condition; otherwise recast. |
| `corresponding` | Obvious pairings. | Delete or name the comparator when ambiguity exists. |
| `respectively` | Long value strings requiring back-mapping. | Name values directly, split the sentence, or use a table. |
| `therefore`, `thus`, `hence` | An inference already obvious from the evidence. | Retain only for a non-obvious logical consequence. |
| Repeated `whereas`, `while`, or `but` frames | Every finding cast as the same binary opposition. | Reserve for central contrasts and vary paragraph structure. |
| `increased` or `decreased` followed by every delta | Narration without a boundary or consequence. | Retain changes that alter classification, mechanism, or scale. |
| Heavy umbrella nouns such as `parameterisation` or `configuration` | Repetition after labels are already defined. | Use the exact label or specific system where technically accurate. |
| `depended on` followed by a factor list | A non-directional summary repeated across sections. | State which factor changed the outcome and in which direction. |
| `These results showed/indicated` | An explanatory tail that restates the preceding evidence. | Delete unless it introduces a mechanism, implication, or limitation. |
| `provided in the Supporting Information` | Repeated vague availability statements. | Use one exact reference per subsection. |
| `can be seen in` | A filter phrase before a visual reference. | Use a direct construction such as `Figure 2 shows`. |
| Repeated factor lists | The full variable list appears in several summaries. | State it once; name only locally relevant factors elsewhere. |
| Exact values across Abstract, Results, and Conclusion | Complete quantitative triplication. | Let Results own detail; retain only decision-defining values in summaries. |
| `rather than` | A scientifically important category distinction. | Retain when it prevents conflation; do not reduce indiscriminately. |

Do not replace precise recurring terms with loose synonyms. Reduce repetition by changing information structure, not merely vocabulary.

## Redundancy audit

Look for two or more elements performing the same function:

- narration and a figure or table;
- Methods setup and Results recap;
- evidence and an explanatory tail;
- full limitation and repeated local caveats;
- Abstract headline and Conclusion recap;
- adjacent section endings and openings;
- multiple examples proving the same claim;
- result and narrator interpretation;
- theme or implication and explicit restatement.

Identify the strongest version, specify what should be cut or merged, and state which function survives.

## Section effectiveness

For each major section, state:

- its primary function;
- what changes because it exists;
- the new evidence, decision, mechanism, or consequence it contributes;
- whether it enters too early or exits too late;
- whether another section performs the same function;
- whether it should remain, shorten, merge, move, expand, or be removed.

Flag sections with no meaningful state change. Do not remove an analytical section merely because its prose is inefficient; first determine whether a rewrite can expose its distinct function.

## Full audit output

For a comprehensive developmental assessment, use:

1. **Executive assessment**: strengths worth preserving, main weaknesses, overall density, three to five highest-value revisions, and conservative/aggressive cut estimates.
2. **Density analysis**: manuscript-, section-, paragraph-, and sentence-level findings, including over-dense and low-yield examples.
3. **Potential cuts**: classified table, consequences, and total ranges.
4. **Cohesion and continuity**: missing links, contradictions, scope drift, setup/payoff, and transition problems.
5. **Repetition and overused language**: frequencies where defensible, locations, intentionality, effect, recommendation, and reduction target.
6. **Redundancy audit**: duplicate functions, strongest version, and material to cut or merge.
7. **Section effectiveness**: function, state change, overlap, and disposition for every major section.
8. **Prioritised revision plan**: high, medium, and low priorities. For each item, state what to change, why it matters, where it occurs, whether it is global or local, and what must be preserved.

Use representative examples rather than listing every occurrence. Explain the likely benefit of every proposed change. Be explicit about uncertainty and personal-taste judgements.

## Rewrite workflow

When the user requests a rewrite rather than an audit:

1. Identify the section's unique function and its relationship to adjacent sections.
2. List the findings, mechanisms, boundaries, qualifications, and numerical anchors that must survive.
3. Remove setup owned by Methods and detail already carried by visuals or Supporting Information.
4. Draft around the governing finding rather than the analysis chronology.
5. Compare the revision with the source for numbers, scope, comparator, tense, terminology, and claim strength.
6. Check that the revision does not duplicate the Abstract or Conclusion.
7. Apply the language, numerical, and typographic audit in the main skill.

If the user requests pasted text only, do not create or modify a document.
